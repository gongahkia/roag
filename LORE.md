# Jomon — Lore and World Reference

> **The Jomon carries sealed objects between divided worlds. Every delivery creates history, whether the courier understands it or not.**

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document defines the setting, tone, narrative rules, and procedural content grammar of **Jomon**. It is not a fixed plot outline. The game must be capable of producing histories that were not written in advance while remaining recognizably part of the same universe.

The document uses four levels of authority:

- **Hard canon** — always true in every campaign.
- **Generated canon** — facts created by a campaign seed and made permanently true within that campaign.
- **Reported truth** — claims made by characters, contracts, institutions, and records; these may be incomplete, mistaken, or deceptive.
- **Protected mystery** — questions the game may investigate but must never answer with one authoritative exposition.

Implementation is not automatically canon. Existing names and mechanics may be retained when they serve this reference, reinterpreted when they contain a useful idea, or replaced when they conflict with it.

## 2. The North Star

**Jomon** is an industrial science-fiction courier roguelike set among disconnected planets, mobile habitats, relay stations, and artificial environments. Humans share the known routes with several established non-human civilizations. Hyperlight vessels can cross interstellar distances, but there is no reliable faster-than-light transmission network and no government capable of guaranteeing passage everywhere.

The **Jomon** is a massive, independently governed vessel following an endless postal route. It negotiates its own contracts, selects its own destinations, and sends one courier at a time into hazardous territory carrying a sealed package whose contents are unknown.

The player is not a chosen saviour. The Jomon is not travelling toward a promised home, and the galaxy is not waiting to be repaired. Worlds continue changing without the player. Governments fall, shortages spread, ecosystems move, workers organize, companies consolidate, non-human polities divide, and rivals pursue their own careers. A delivery can alter those histories, but it cannot make the universe revolve around the courier.

The game's emotional centre is the conflict between:

- connection and contamination;
- duty and knowledge;
- institutional continuity and individual mortality;
- political neutrality and material consequence;
- the declared purpose of a package and what it actually does.

## 3. Hard Canon

The following facts are immutable:

1. The vessel is named **Jomon**. It is never called *Jomon Voyager*.
2. The Jomon is a massive long-range carrier, home, workplace, archive, and political community.
3. It operates independently. No universal postal authority grants it protection or commands its route.
4. Its route is endless and evolving. There is no canonical final destination or terminal victory.
5. One expedition follows one courier attempting one contracted delivery.
6. Contract packages are sealed, and their contents are normally unknown to the courier.
7. A courier can break the seal, but doing so is a meaningful contract violation with material consequences.
8. Couriers can die permanently. The Jomon, surviving crew, institutional memory, and simulated worlds persist.
9. Humans coexist with multiple established non-human civilizations. No species is culturally or politically unified.
10. Hyperlight travel transports vessels and physical cargo between systems; dependable interstellar real-time communication does not exist.
11. The known worlds contain industrial remnants left by civilizations that no longer operate them.
12. The setting contains no literal magic. Apparently supernatural events must have material causes, even when those causes remain beyond present understanding.
13. Worlds and major characters act without the player. Their histories are causal, persistent, and capable of reaching conclusions.
14. ASCII and detailed top-down tiles are equally valid renderings of the same world state. Neither is a different canon.

## 4. Known Space

“Known space” is not a bordered nation or a complete astronomical survey. It is the set of routes, ports, coordinates, warnings, and reciprocal agreements that a traveller can presently use.

Its shape is irregular. A nearby star may be unreachable because no safe hyperlight approach is known, while a distant industrial port may be one established passage away. Routes drift, collapse, reopen, or become politically unusable. Maps therefore describe relationships rather than simple distance.

There has been no single galaxy-wide collapse. Different regions have experienced expansion, isolation, war, ecological failure, technological regression, and recovery at different times. One world may operate orbital foundries while its neighbour cannibalizes pressure pumps built by a civilization that vanished thousands of years earlier.

No reliable master chronology exists. Local calendars disagree, transit creates administrative ambiguity, and records are frequently copied through institutions with political interests. The Jomon maintains its own sequential **Route Reckoning**, but this is a custody and scheduling system, not a universal date.

### 4.1 Hyperlight Passage

Hyperlight travel is a physical transport process. A vessel must reach a viable approach volume, calculate a route through changing gravitational conditions, survive entry stress, and emerge within a bounded destination region. Larger vessels require more energy and have fewer safe approaches, but they can carry the shielding, repair capacity, and redundant systems needed for long route chains.

Hyperlight has four setting consequences:

- **Information travels aboard vessels.** Tightbeam and radio remain limited by light speed. A physically carried archive may arrive decades before a transmitted copy.
- **Routes are infrastructure.** Navigation observations, approach beacons, fuel, maintenance knowledge, and political access all have value.
- **Isolation is normal.** A world can be technologically sophisticated while knowing little about events elsewhere.
- **Arrival is an intervention.** A large vessel changes prices, expectations, faction plans, and local security merely by appearing.

The precise physics of hyperlight is a protected mystery. The game may establish operating constraints, failure signatures, and engineering procedures, but should not reduce it to a complete textbook explanation.

### 4.2 The Route Board

The Jomon plans movement through the **Route Board**, a strategic map assembled from its own observations, purchased records, public notices, intercepted traffic, and reports carried by other vessels. It shows reachable destinations and partial intelligence rather than objective forecasts.

A route entry may identify:

- probable contracts;
- fuel and maintenance demands;
- environmental and political hazards;
- known factions or individual rivals;
- opportunities for repairs, recruitment, trade, or salvage;
- the age and reliability of the information;
- events likely to conclude if the Jomon travels elsewhere first.

Route selection is therefore both logistical and political. Choosing one destination consumes time in every other active history. A short profitable passage may strengthen an abusive port authority; a dangerous detour may preserve a settlement but leave another contract to expire. The Board should communicate risk and possibility without reducing the future to exact percentages.

## 5. Civilizations and Personhood

Humans are widespread but are neither the original owners of every inhabited world nor the default against which all other people are measured. Several non-human species maintain cities, industries, legal systems, shipping interests, and interplanetary relationships. Mixed settlements are common along major routes.

Species does not determine faction. Every major people must contain competing classes, professions, regions, ideologies, and institutions. “The humans want” or “the aliens believe” is never sufficient worldbuilding.

The following are the initial civilization anchors. Their names and material characteristics are canon; individual cultures derived from them are generated or authored separately.

### 5.1 Humans

Human populations range from relatively unmodified communities to lineages adapted for radiation, pressure, low gravity, chemical exposure, or long-duration transit. Cybernetics and engineered organs are ordinary in some regions and prohibited in others.

Humanity has no common government, religion, origin story, or political project. Human institutions include municipal governments, company jurisdictions, shipboard compacts, worker federations, military administrations, research enclaves, refugee flotillas, and family-owned industrial stations.

### 5.2 The Orra

An Orra person is a coordinated colony of several specialized bodies linked through chemical exchange and short-range signalling. Some bodies perform locomotion, some memory, some reproduction, and some technical work. A person may temporarily divide to complete separate tasks and later reintegrate.

This makes familiar legal concepts difficult. An Orra individual can lose a body without dying, produce a legally disputed derivative, or merge memories with another person. Orra contracts specify continuity, authorized instances, and which bodies are empowered to receive custody.

Orra industries are strongest in biochemistry, closed-cycle agriculture, solvents, sealants, and living filtration systems. Their technologies are biological in manufacture but industrial in operation: vats, conduits, controlled growth, inspection regimes, and replaceable tissue assemblies. They are not mystical organisms and do not possess collective telepathy.

### 5.3 The Taal

The Taal evolved under high pressure in a dense, chemically active atmosphere. Outside compatible environments they use pressure garments, mobile chambers, or sealed work frames. They communicate through a combination of low-frequency vibration, touch, and visible pressure changes translated imperfectly by common interfaces.

Taal civilizations are known for ceramics, pressure engineering, fluid machinery, structural damping, and slow-cycle industrial planning. Their built environments often remain functional under conditions lethal to humans and Orra.

Taal institutions frequently distinguish between a decision, its physical implementation, and responsibility for the resulting pressure change. Translation can make this sound evasive when it is actually a precise division of liability.

### 5.4 The Vey

The Vey are electrosensory, radially organized organisms whose natural bodies cannot perform most heavy industrial work. They use fabricated mobility frames from early adulthood. These frames are not vehicles or optional prosthetics; they are part of ordinary Vey personhood and may be repaired, inherited in part, or rebuilt over a lifetime.

Vey societies have sophisticated power systems, sensor manufacturing, and electromagnetic materials. They draw sharp distinctions between bodily continuity, memory continuity, and registered legal identity. A stolen frame component can be property, evidence, or part of a person depending on its use and history.

Vey are biological people, not machines, artificial intelligences, or a vanished precursor species.

### 5.5 Additional Peoples

Other intelligent species may be introduced when they add a distinct material condition, social problem, and relationship to transport. New species must not exist solely as an enemy type or visual novelty. Each requires:

- a compatible environment;
- a bodily account of perception and labour;
- multiple internal cultures;
- ordinary civilian life;
- an industrial relationship to other species;
- meaningful variation between individuals.

## 6. Orphan Works

Across known space are machines, structures, artificial ecologies, buried transport systems, and planetary installations whose operating civilizations are absent. Engineers call these **Orphan Works**.

The term makes no claim that all such systems share one builder. Material analysis indicates multiple technological lineages separated by enormous spans of time. Some works have been repaired by later cultures so many times that their original layers cannot be cleanly distinguished.

Orphan Works are not magical ruins. They have inputs, outputs, tolerances, waste products, maintenance cycles, and failure modes. Their mystery comes from scale, missing context, inaccessible fabrication methods, and purposes that cannot be reconstructed from surviving components.

Examples include:

- weather-regulation towers that continue optimizing for an extinct biosphere;
- mantle heat exchangers larger than inhabited cities;
- transit apertures with no reachable counterpart;
- automated quarries producing standardized objects nobody can identify;
- oceanic pump networks whose shutdown would destroy the settlements built around them;
- dormant fabrication lines that reject all known control languages;
- artificial organisms that maintain industrial sites without recognizing current inhabitants.

Present-day cultures may build customs, laws, and political legitimacy around Orphan Works, but the lore should describe those relationships in material terms. Avoid shrines, spells, curses, spirits, divine selection, and ritual magic. A maintenance ceremony may exist as workplace tradition; it does not make the machine supernatural.

### 6.1 Protected Questions

The following must remain unresolved:

- whether any builder of the oldest Orphan Works still exists;
- whether some disconnected works were once parts of a single network;
- whether hyperlight passage was discovered independently or inherited;
- whether a functioning work can meaningfully distinguish its intended operators from later users;
- how many technological civilizations have disappeared from known space.

Clues may support incompatible models. No codex entry, narrator, final boss, or secret ending should settle them all.

## 7. The Jomon

The Jomon is a city-scale interstellar carrier rebuilt over generations of operation. Its hull contains habitation districts, machine spaces, pressure farms, fabrication halls, cargo vaults, hangars, archives, medical decks, translation facilities, and sealed regions that may be inaccessible during parts of a campaign.

Its original commissioning records are incomplete and politically unimportant to most current crew. Whatever it was first built to do, it now survives by transporting objects, information, specialists, and occasionally passengers between places that cannot depend on anyone else arriving.

The ship is neither pristine nor uniformly designed. Repairs from different worlds remain visible. Human access ladders meet Taal pressure locks. Orra filtration tissue grows through industrial housings. Vey sensor laminates cover older ceramic armour. The Jomon's material history is a record of whom it has trusted and what it has been forced to become.

### 7.1 Independence

The Jomon belongs to no interstellar government or postal network. Its authority is contractual and local.

At each destination, the crew must negotiate:

- recognition of its seals;
- permission to enter controlled territory;
- liability for the courier and package;
- inspection limits;
- landing access;
- payment medium;
- rules for recovery if the courier dies;
- whether the Jomon is considered neutral, commercial, hostile, or unclassified.

Some ports recognize decades of reliable service. Others remember a package the Jomon opened, a blockade it crossed, or a faction it enabled. Neutrality is therefore a reputation the ship performs, not a legal fact.

### 7.2 Shipboard Government

The Jomon is governed through the **Ship Compact**, an evolving body of operational rules, custody precedents, labour agreements, and emergency powers. No single captain owns the vessel or speaks for every deck.

The exact offices may vary with the campaign, but the ship always requires responsibility for:

- route selection and navigation;
- contracts and external negotiation;
- cargo custody;
- engineering and life support;
- medical care and modification review;
- archives and translation;
- security;
- provisioning;
- crew representation.

Crew politics should arise from material interests. Engineers may oppose a profitable route because the drive cannot survive it. A habitation bloc may demand refugee intake. Custody staff may refuse cargo that exposes the whole vessel to seizure. Couriers may organize against being treated as disposable.

The player shapes the Jomon's institutional identity through repeated decisions rather than selecting a morality meter.

### 7.3 The General Manifest

The Jomon's persistent record is the **General Manifest**. It includes more than cargo:

- accepted and refused contracts;
- custody transfers;
- crew membership and status;
- courier deaths and missing persons;
- disputes and rulings;
- route observations;
- known hazards;
- repairs and incorporated technologies;
- debts, sanctions, and standing permissions;
- reports from visited worlds.

The Manifest is not perfectly truthful. Entries can be sealed, amended, disputed, or falsified. Its value comes from continuity: it is the closest thing the Jomon has to a memory that outlives individual crew members.

### 7.4 The Jomon as Persistent Protagonist

Couriers may become powerful, beloved, compromised, or infamous, but none is guaranteed survival. The ship preserves their effects:

- equipment returned to stores;
- modifications studied or prohibited;
- relationships inherited by later couriers;
- memorial entries;
- unfinished obligations;
- enemies who recognize the Jomon even when the original courier is dead.

The ship should gradually acquire scars, customs, capabilities, and political positions unique to its campaign.

## 8. Couriers

A courier is a crew member entrusted to establish physical custody at the source, carry the package through a hostile route, and complete or formally terminate custody at the destination.

Courier work combines logistics, survival, negotiation, and violence. It is not automatically honourable. Some people volunteer because the work grants status or mobility. Others owe service, need money, seek access to planets, want distance from the ship, or believe delivery is the only institution worth maintaining.

One run begins when a courier accepts custody and ends when the package is delivered, lost, destroyed, opened, abandoned, transferred, or made impossible to deliver.

### 8.1 Courier Continuity

A surviving courier retains:

- learned techniques;
- injuries and disabilities;
- biological or mechanical modifications;
- personal relationships;
- contractual reputation;
- knowledge acquired during expeditions;
- equipment successfully returned to the Jomon.

These are not a clean progression tree. A modification may improve combat ability while creating medical dependency, legal exclusion, or conflict with the crew. An injury may close one route and open another through local recognition. Equipment can be reassigned, damaged, confiscated, or inherited.

### 8.2 Courier Death

Death is permanent for the individual and consequential for everyone else.

When a courier dies, the simulation determines:

- who witnessed it;
- whether the body and package were recovered;
- who took possession of their equipment;
- whether a rival claims responsibility;
- how the contract is resolved;
- what the Jomon records;
- how surviving crew react;
- whether another courier inherits the relationship.

Death should create history, not merely erase a build.

## 9. Contracts and Sealed Packages

The package is the central narrative object. It must never be reduced to generic units of “cargo.”

### 9.1 Contract Structure

Every delivery contract defines, truthfully or otherwise:

- sender identity or authorized intermediary;
- recipient identity, role, or qualifying condition;
- source and intended destination;
- custody chain;
- deadline or delivery window;
- declared mass and handling class;
- permitted inspection methods;
- prohibited actions;
- payment, collateral, and penalties;
- rules for death, incapacity, refusal, or destination failure;
- political jurisdiction claimed by the contract.

The Jomon can negotiate these terms. It can demand higher payment, refuse inspection clauses, substitute a courier, request route access, or decline the work. Negotiation itself can change relationships before the package leaves the ship.

### 9.2 What the Courier Knows

The courier normally knows the contract and the exterior behaviour of the package, not its contents.

Observable characteristics may include:

- weight and balance;
- heat production;
- vibration or internal motion;
- power draw;
- shielding;
- pressure requirements;
- sound or electromagnetic leakage;
- biological activity declarations;
- seal manufacturer and custody history;
- discrepancies between the package and its paperwork.

Good package writing creates informed uncertainty. The player should form hypotheses without receiving a simple “mystery box” prompt.

### 9.3 The Seal

A custody seal records tampering, environmental excursions, and authorized transfers. Seals vary by culture and technology, but all must provide a clear game state: intact, stressed, compromised, opened, resealed, or destroyed.

The player may open a package when physically possible. Opening it can provide knowledge, tools, danger, leverage, or proof of deception. It also changes the legal and political meaning of the delivery even if the contents are returned perfectly.

Opening a package is not inherently evil. Keeping it sealed is not inherently good.

### 9.4 Package Content Families

The following families guide authored content. They are not labels always shown to the player.

- **Civil continuity:** medicines, machine parts, voting records, identity archives, payroll keys, repair instructions.
- **Political force:** treaties, evidence, succession documents, command credentials, sanctions, lists of informants.
- **Industrial leverage:** catalysts, fabrication seeds, control modules, rare tools, process samples.
- **Living custody:** organisms, embryos, symbiotic bodies, legal persons in transport states, quarantine material.
- **Dangerous knowledge:** route solutions, engineering exploits, intercepted records, translation models.
- **Personal consequence:** remains, inheritance, private messages, proof of kinship, objects whose value exists only to the recipient.
- **Unknown machinery:** components removed from Orphan Works, dormant devices, interfaces with disputed purpose.

Not every package should threaten a planet. Ordinary dependencies make extraordinary packages believable.

### 9.5 Resolution

A package can be:

- delivered to the intended recipient;
- delivered to a disputed or replacement recipient;
- transferred under renegotiated custody;
- stolen;
- confiscated;
- opened and still delivered;
- opened and redirected;
- abandoned;
- destroyed;
- lost with the courier;
- returned to the Jomon;
- rendered meaningless by events at the destination.

The simulation must record who believes what happened. Material truth and reported truth may diverge.

## 10. Worlds That Continue Without the Player

The world simulation is a source of narrative, not background decoration.

Every major actor has:

- needs;
- resources;
- relationships;
- obligations;
- current knowledge;
- goals;
- methods;
- tolerances for risk;
- memories of consequential events.

Actors pursue goals while the player explores, waits, travels, or chooses another route. Their actions change control, supply, ecology, employment, migration, access, and personal status.

### 10.1 Simulation Resolution

The game may simulate different places at different levels of detail:

- The active expedition receives tile-level movement, combat, hazards, and encounters.
- The current planet receives settlement, faction, ecological, and logistical events.
- Distant known worlds receive lower-frequency historical events derived from persistent actors and pressures.

This is a technical abstraction, not a fictional difference. Outcomes must remain causal at every level.

### 10.2 Visible History

The player should encounter history through changes such as:

- a former checkpoint becoming a refugee market;
- a worker the courier rescued becoming a union leader;
- a neglected settlement being evacuated;
- an imported organism altering local industry;
- a contract recipient dying before arrival;
- a rival acquiring rank, injury, equipment, or dependants;
- a faction using an earlier delivery to justify new authority;
- a route warning written by a dead courier saving their successor.

If a simulated event cannot eventually influence a decision, relationship, location, or report, it is probably unnecessary.

### 10.3 Endless but Not Aimless

The campaign has no mandatory ending, but its histories must conclude at smaller scales:

- a courier's career ends;
- a government survives or falls;
- a strike is won, broken, or transformed;
- a rival reconciles, succeeds, disappears, or dies;
- a world becomes habitable or is abandoned;
- a chain of packages resolves;
- a crew dispute changes the Ship Compact;
- a district of the Jomon is rebuilt or sealed.

The player remains engaged through overlapping finite arcs inside an endless route.

## 11. Procedural World Grammar

Planets are assembled procedurally from authored components. Procedural generation must create causal coherence, not arbitrary combinations.

Every inhabited destination requires:

1. **Material environment** — gravity, atmosphere, temperature, radiation, terrain, and dominant hazards.
2. **Ecology** — native, engineered, imported, or absent; including who depends on it.
3. **Settlement logic** — why people live here and what keeps them alive.
4. **Population** — multiple species, classes, professions, and communities where appropriate.
5. **Industrial base** — what is extracted, grown, fabricated, repaired, or transported.
6. **Critical dependency** — something the settlement cannot locally replace.
7. **Power structure** — who controls access to that dependency and how.
8. **Competing actors** — at least two groups with incompatible but understandable goals.
9. **Route relationship** — why the place needs or resents outside contact.
10. **Present crisis** — a changing condition that produces contracts and movement.
11. **Historical residue** — prior decisions, abandoned infrastructure, or Orphan Works affecting the present.
12. **Ordinary life** — evidence that inhabitants do more than wait for conflict.

### 11.1 Culture Construction

An authored culture module should define:

- environmental adaptation;
- labour organization;
- family or continuity structures;
- property and custody assumptions;
- attitudes toward bodily modification;
- conflict-resolution practices;
- architecture and interfaces;
- food, rest, and maintenance;
- relationship to outsiders;
- internal disagreement.

No planet should be a single “hat.” A mining planet also has childcare, waste management, entertainment, illness, private ambition, and political dissent.

### 11.2 Place Construction

Every tactical place should communicate function before lore exposition. Examples include:

- pressure refinery;
- orbital tether foundation;
- abandoned coolant farm;
- cargo interchange;
- worker habitat;
- quarantine depot;
- dismantled shipyard;
- flooded data centre;
- atmospheric processing trench;
- Orphan Work maintenance access;

Use industrial nouns and material evidence. Avoid generic temples, cursed forests, magic tombs, spirit paths, barrows, and ritual chambers.

## 12. Factions

There are no permanent species-wide factions and no universal good or evil alignment.

Each faction is generated or authored from:

- constituency;
- material dependency;
- governing method;
- source of legitimacy;
- public objective;
- internal division;
- external rival;
- relationship to transport;
- threshold for violence;
- memory of the Jomon.

Useful faction forms include port authorities, extraction companies, municipal services, military administrations, worker federations, convoy associations, research consortia, quarantine offices, salvage cooperatives, creditor courts, refugee councils, and criminal logistics networks.

The existing prototype's generic salvager, relay, settler, hostile, and ship factions are not canon. They should be replaced by specific regional institutions produced through this grammar.

## 13. Rivals and Recurring Characters

A rival is any persistent actor whose goals repeatedly intersect with the Jomon or one of its couriers. Rivalry is a relationship, not an enemy class.

Potential rivals include:

- another courier;
- a customs examiner;
- a route inspector;
- a military officer;
- a salvage claimant;
- a recipient;
- a labour organizer;
- a company investigator;
- a local guide;
- a survivor harmed by an earlier delivery.

Every persistent rival requires:

- personal goal;
- institutional position;
- relationships with other actors;
- memories of specific encounters;
- material resources;
- an opinion of the Jomon distinct from their opinion of the current courier;
- capacity to gain rank, injury, equipment, allies, dependants, or disgrace;
- at least one path other than combat.

An elite or climactic opponent should emerge from this history whenever possible. A port officer who closes routes, survives an encounter, receives a promotion, and later intercepts the package is stronger content than a randomly enlarged enemy.

## 14. Technology, Equipment, and Modification

Technology is industrial, repaired, jurisdictional, and culturally specific.

Equipment descriptions should identify manufacture, use, maintenance, and previous ownership. Prefer:

- ceramic cutter;
- pressure-rated shield;
- coolant injector;
- survey drone;
- induction spear;
- breaching line;
- field sealant;
- sensor laminate;
- load frame;
- emergency circulator.

Avoid spell, charm, curse, spirit, rune, blessing, sacred relic, and enchanted weapon.

### 14.1 Run Builds

Run-specific power comes from equipment, artifacts, learned techniques, injuries, and risky modification. Effects may stack into surprising combinations, but every effect should have a material fiction.

Examples:

- a heat-recovery unit converts environmental exposure into battery charge;
- a Vey sensor layer causes multiple detection effects to share targets;
- an Orra filtration graft neutralizes one toxin while creating dependence on nutrient gel;
- a Taal damping brace shortens stagger but increases movement commitment;
- a recovered control shard changes the behaviour of nearby Orphan machinery.

Power should introduce new dependencies, liabilities, or social consequences rather than only increasing numbers.

### 14.2 Orphan Artifacts

Artifacts recovered from Orphan Works can produce effects that present science cannot fully manufacture. Their operation must still be consistent and inspectable. They are dangerous because their tolerances and objectives are unknown, not because they are magical.

## 15. Danger and Escalation

Elapsed time increases danger during a delivery, but the world should appear to respond rather than merely scale statistics.

As time passes:

- weather fronts move;
- shifts change;
- patrols receive reports;
- predators or maintenance organisms become active;
- rival teams converge;
- checkpoints close;
- industrial systems enter dangerous cycles;
- the recipient relocates;
- an elite actor gains jurisdiction or preparation time.

Enemies use readable attack windups and threatened tiles. Combat is deliberate, spatial, and lethal. The fiction should support telegraphs through posture, targeting beams, pressure changes, mechanical charge cycles, sound, or visible terrain failure.

## 16. Narrative Truth and Delivery

The setting operates through three simultaneous kinds of truth:

1. **Material truth:** what physically exists and what the simulation records as having happened.
2. **Cultural truth:** how a community interprets that material reality.
3. **Reported truth:** what the Jomon is told through contracts, maps, witnesses, propaganda, and archives.

The player rarely receives material truth directly. Conflicting accounts should be meaningful, not arbitrary. A sender lies for a reason. A witness misunderstands according to available evidence. A translation loses distinctions that matter to another species.

Lore is primarily delivered through:

- contract clauses;
- package labels and physical symptoms;
- route intelligence;
- crew arguments;
- inspection reports;
- environmental change;
- industrial spaces;
- rival memory;
- local public notices;
- maintenance records;
- custody disputes;
- the General Manifest;
- consequences visible on return.

Codex entries should record evidence and competing interpretations rather than dispense omniscient answers.

## 17. Tone and Language

The dominant tone is:

- oppressive and materially dangerous;
- lonely and strange;
- restrained rather than melodramatic;
- capable of dry institutional absurdity;
- occasionally and sincerely hopeful.

Humour comes from procedure colliding with reality:

- a customs category that cannot describe an Orra recipient;
- a liability form updated while the building is collapsing;
- an automated condolence issued before death is confirmed;
- a package accruing storage fees during a civil war;
- two jurisdictions each insisting the other must inspect dangerous cargo.

Humour must not make death, disability, poverty, or non-human difference into a punchline.

Hope should be local and expensive. A delivery may keep one pressure plant operating, reunite two people, provide evidence that frees a worker, or preserve a community archive. It need not save a planet to matter.

### 17.1 Prose Rules

Prefer concrete observation:

> The case draws six watts from the courier's pack and is three degrees warmer than its declaration allows.

Over vague portent:

> The ancient parcel hums with forbidden power.

Prefer institutional conflict:

> The recipient named in the contract is dead. Three offices possess valid continuation orders.

Over binary morality:

> Choose whether to help the good faction or the evil faction.

Prefer material horror:

> The filtration tissue has learned to treat human blood as industrial waste.

Over supernatural horror:

> A curse haunts the sacred machine.

## 18. Visual Canon

ASCII and detailed tile art depict the same entities, spaces, hazards, and events. Players can switch freely without changing mechanics or lore.

Both renderers must preserve:

- occupancy and collision;
- faction and relationship cues;
- attack telegraphs;
- hazard intensity;
- package state;
- interactive machinery;
- visibility and uncertainty;
- silhouettes of humans and non-human bodies.

ASCII is a deliberate art direction, not a debug fallback. It should use a consistent glyph and colour grammar supported by portraits, interface panels, sound, and animation. Tile art adds material texture without revealing information hidden in ASCII mode.

The renderer switch is not diegetic and requires no explanation in the lore.

## 19. Initial Content Anchors

The following existing names may be used as provisional authored anchors without becoming universal structures:

- **Ari Venn** — security specialist and potential courier.
- **Mika Sol** — systems specialist and potential courier.
- **Cato Ren** — xenoarchivist and potential courier.
- **Dara Ilyan** — field medic and potential courier.
- **Kestrel**, **Nerida**, **Orison**, **Halcyon**, and **Borealis** — suitable roots for particular places, stations, or route regions.

Their current prototype characterization is not binding. If retained, each person needs motives, relationships, political interests, and a reason to risk courier work.

The survey ethic is canon in revised form:

> **A route report is a promise to the next courier.**

Surveying is not the Jomon's ultimate mission, but accurate reports are essential to delivery, survival, and institutional memory.

## 20. Illustrative World Seed: Nerida Pressure Chain

This example demonstrates the content grammar. It is usable content but may be revised during implementation.

### Material Environment

Nerida is an oceanic world with a toxic surface atmosphere. Humans and Taal inhabit linked pressure settlements anchored along a submerged industrial chain. The settlements depend on pumps integrated into an Orphan Work that regulates circulation through a section of the planetary crust.

### Industrial Dependency

The pump network requires ceramic control vanes no local facility can manufacture. A Taal cooperative can repair damaged vanes, but the human-controlled port authority controls imported feedstock and departure access.

### Active Actors

- **Nerida Port Continuity Office:** claims emergency authority over all imported machinery.
- **Closure Eight Cooperative:** a mixed Taal-human maintenance workforce seeking independent custody of the pump controls.
- **Blue Intake Residents' Board:** represents habitats that will flood first if output falls.
- **Examiner Iren Vos:** a customs official building a career through strict enforcement and selective confiscation.

### Present Crisis

One pump bank is degrading. The Continuity Office claims sabotage. Closure Eight claims deferred maintenance and falsified inspection records. Both accounts contain evidence. Pressure shifts have opened a hazardous maintenance approach populated by autonomous repair organisms that now classify inhabited structures as obstructions.

### Delivery Contract

The Jomon accepts a sealed case from an off-world fabrication broker. The declared recipient is “the licensed operator of Pump Bank Four at time of transfer.” The case is mass-consistent with replacement calibration hardware, draws unexpected power, and carries two incompatible customs registrations.

The material truth is generated from several possibilities, including:

- legitimate calibration hardware;
- root control credentials capable of transferring practical ownership;
- fabricated evidence designed to implicate Closure Eight;
- a Vey sensor component that reveals structural failure elsewhere;
- a device removed from another Orphan Work with uncertain compatibility.

The courier can deliver under the original wording, renegotiate the recipient, open the case, surrender it to customs, or abandon the route. Every resolution changes control, relationships, and Nerida's future state.

### Rival Development

Examiner Iren Vos remembers compliance, deception, injury, confiscation, and public humiliation. Vos may become an ally against the Continuity Office, a promoted regional inspector, a private investigator, or a determined enemy of the Jomon. Their development depends on events rather than a fixed villain role.

## 22. Content Guardrails

Do not introduce:

- Jomon Voyager;
- a chosen courier;
- a universal postal service;
- a final destination that ends the route;
- a single species-wide culture;
- literal magic;
- ancient gods or prophecy as factual explanations;
- generic good and evil factions;
- packages that are always weapons, monsters, or galaxy-ending devices;
- lore whose only purpose is to justify loot;
- simulation events invisible to all meaningful player decisions;
- an omniscient codex that resolves protected mysteries.

Every major piece of content should answer at least one of these questions:

1. How does it complicate carrying the package?
2. How does it change the courier carrying it?
3. How does it change the Jomon that sent them?
4. How does it change the world receiving—or failing to receive—it?

If it answers none of them, it probably belongs outside the game.

## 23. Final Creative Principle

The universe of **Jomon** is not held together by destiny. It is held together imperfectly by labour: navigation, maintenance, translation, negotiation, memory, and the dangerous physical act of carrying one sealed object from one person to another.

The package may be ordinary. The route may be incomprehensible. The courier may die. The recipient may already be gone.

The delivery still creates history.
